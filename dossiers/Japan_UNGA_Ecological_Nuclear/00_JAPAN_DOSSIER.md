## 4. JAPAN'S DOCTRINE (interests → red lines → tradeables)

### National interests at stake
1. **Moral authority as the world's only atomic-bombed nation** — the core of Japan's foreign policy identity; defending it is non-negotiable.
2. **Extended deterrence + NPT-based order** — Japan will NOT sign the TPNW; it needs the US alliance and the NPT regime intact. Any clause attacking nuclear-weapon states so hard that they walk kills the treaty Japan depends on.
3. **Regional security** — DPRK's nuclear/missile program and China's opaque buildup are Japan's live threats; Japan wants transparency and verification, especially on DPRK (ecological angle: Punggye-ri collapse risk) and China (fastest-growing arsenal).
4. **Environmental credibility** — Fukushima forces Japan to support strong safety/transparency standards (IAEA-centered), while defending its own discharge decision as standards-compliant.
5. **Pacific neighborhood** — Japan must court Pacific island states (Marshall Islands, Fiji, Palau, Tuvalu, Samoa), whose waters absorbed the tests; they are Japan's natural partners and potential attackers on Fukushima.

### Red lines (do not trade)
- No language implying Japan will join the TPNW or abandon extended deterrence.
- No clause that legitimizes new testing under any guise.
- No singling-out of Japan on civil nuclear energy; keep the agenda on *proliferation* (weapons), per the agenda wording.
- No commitment to specific reparations figures for test sites (Japan is not the responsible party; it channels remediation via multilateral funds instead).

### Tradeable items
- Support for stronger "capacity-building/technical assistance" language (Global South asks).
- Support for recognizing affected communities and legacy sites broadly (wins Marshall Islands, Kazakhstan, Pacific bloc) — costs Japan nothing.
- Accept softer language on "disarmament education" and "youth engagement" (Nihon Hidankyo-friendly) in exchange for hard mechanisms on monitoring/remediation.

## 6. THREE LEADABLE POLICY PACKAGES (FAMMS-tested)

### P1 — Global Radioactive Legacy Remediation & Transparency Fund ("Legacy Fund")
- **Feasible:** voluntary multi-donor fund (Japan, EU states, US, Gulf states), managed via IAEA/UNDP channels; phased pilot projects.
- **Authority:** GA resolution requests the Secretary-General + IAEA Director-General to establish it; states contribute voluntarily.
- **Measurable:** independent baseline surveys of legacy sites (Marshall Islands, Semipalatinsk, French Polynesia, Central Asia tailings, Punggye-ri risk zone, Arctic dumping areas); public data portal; 5-year review.
- **Money:** voluntary assessed-adjacent contributions; Japan signals leadership by pledging (you can announce a symbolic opening pledge as the delegate's "strategy" — verify with your chair before naming amounts).
- **Safeguards:** host-state consent; IAEA safety standards; independent monitoring; transparency portal.
- **Political win:** every affected community in the room supports this; no nuclear-weapon state can oppose "cleaning up legacy contamination" without looking cruel.

### P2 — Transboundary Radiation Monitoring & Early Notification Protocol ("No-Borders Protocol")
- **Feasible:** extends the existing 1986 Convention on Early Notification / Assistance frameworks; uses CTBTO IMS data + national monitoring; regional data-sharing nodes (Asia-Pacific first).
- **Authority:** GA calls upon states to accede to the 1986 conventions and to share real-time data; requests IAEA/CTBTO cooperation.
- **Measurable:** shared public radiation data portal; joint emergency-response exercises; annual reporting.
- **Money:** small secretariat cost; voluntary fund.
- **Safeguards:** data sovereignty (states retain control of national data); open access for science.
- **Political win:** Japan's own Fukushima experience makes Japan the natural author; even China/Russia can sign "better monitoring" language (they dislike it quietly but cannot oppose it publicly).

### P3 — Testing Taboo & Disarmament Reinforcement ("No More Lucky Dragons")
- **Feasible:** GA resolution reaffirming the moratorium on nuclear testing; calling for CTBT entry into force; mandating an independent environmental impact assessment for ANY future nuclear test explosion; supporting the NPT intersessional process after the 2026 failure.
- **Authority:** GA recommendation; political commitment.
- **Measurable:** number of states ratifying CTBT; EIA reports published; NPT working group meetings.
- **Money:** minimal; support for CTBTO.
- **Safeguards:** does not reopen NPT bargains; framed as "the environment clause the NPT never had."
- **Political win:** the Lucky Dragon story + Punggye-ri collapse + resumption-of-testing reports make this the emotional and factual center of the committee. Japan owns it absolutely.

**Resolution architecture:** Preamble (Hiroshima/Nagasaki, Lucky Dragon, 2,000+ tests, Nature Food science, 2026 RevCon failure, Stockholm/Rio/UNCLOS/London instruments, SDGs) → Operatives (Legacy Fund; No-Borders Protocol; EIA-before-test; CTBT call; NPT follow-up; capacity building; review clause).

## 7. SPEECHES

### 7.1 GSL 90 seconds — Version A ("Principled") — target ~205 words

> Honourable Chair, distinguished delegates.
>
> Japan stands before you as the only nation to have suffered atomic bombing — and as the only nation whose fishermen were poisoned by fallout from a nuclear test eighty miles away. The *Lucky Dragon*, March 1954. We learned the lesson this agenda is asking you to learn: **radioactivity does not recognize borders.**
>
> More than two thousand nuclear tests have scarred our planet. The Marshall Islands, Polynesia, Kazakhstan, and our own Pacific waters still carry that burden. And the threat is not history — it is tomorrow. The 2026 NPT Review Conference ended without consensus. The third consecutive failure. The testing moratorium is under open pressure. Science warns that even a regional nuclear war could starve more than two billion people; a full-scale exchange, more than five.
>
> Proliferation multiplies every one of these risks. So Japan calls for three concrete steps. **First**, a Global Radioactive Legacy Remediation Fund — assessed, verified, and transparent. **Second**, a binding protocol for transboundary radiation monitoring and early notification — because the next contamination will not ask for a passport. **Third**, universal reaffirmation of the testing moratorium and entry into force of the CTBT.
>
> My delegation will work with every delegation — nuclear and non-nuclear alike — to turn this assessment into action. We owe it to the hibakusha. We owe it to the oceans. And we owe it to the future. Thank you.

*(~210 words ≈ 88–92 s at 140 wpm — trim if needed.)*

### 7.2 GSL 90 seconds — Version B ("Hard edge") — for when the room needs waking up

> Honourable Chair.
>
> Let me begin with arithmetic, not sentiment. Nine states possess over twelve thousand nuclear warheads. Russia and the United States alone hold ninety percent. China's arsenal is growing by one hundred warheads per year — the fastest on Earth. And for the first time in a generation, the era of reductions has ended. All nine nuclear-armed states are modernizing.
>
> What does that mean for this agenda? Every new warhead is a new source of risk — of testing, of accident, of contamination that no border will stop. The scientific panel of this committee has heard the evidence: even a regional nuclear exchange could cut global food production and starve billions. Nuclear proliferation is not a security issue with ecological side effects. It is an ecological threat with security roots.
>
> That is why Japan will not accept "assessment without action." We propose: a legacy remediation fund for the contaminated lands of the Pacific, Central Asia, and beyond; a transboundary radiation early-warning protocol; and a global pledge that no state will test — ever again — without an independent environmental impact assessment.
>
> Japan has walked this path for eighty-one years. We invite you to walk it with us. Thank you.

### 7.3 Moderated caucus 60 s variants (use after you pass the motions)

**Mod A — "Legacy contamination of past testing" (~150 words):**
> The history of nuclear testing is written on the bodies of oceans and islands. The Marshall Islands absorbed sixty-seven tests between 1946 and 1958. Kazakhstan's Semipalatinsk field endured four hundred and fifty-six. My own nation's fishermen were contaminated in 1954 — eighty miles outside a declared danger zone. These are not distant events: the DPRK's last test, in 2017, was followed by reports that the mountain itself collapsed, with unknown leakage risk. The committee cannot assess the ecological threat of proliferation while ignoring the contamination already in the ground and the sea. Japan proposes an internationally verified baseline survey of legacy test sites and uranium legacy sites, published openly, as the first operative commitment of our resolution. We invite the Marshall Islands, Kazakhstan, and all affected states to co-author this clause with us.

**Mod B — "The testing taboo under pressure" (~150 words):**
> Between 1945 and 1996, the world conducted more than two thousand nuclear tests. Since 1996, fewer than a dozen — because the moratorium held. Today it is under pressure: the CTBT has still not entered into force, one nuclear state has withdrawn its ratification, and reports in late 2025 of plans to resume testing after three decades should alarm every delegation here. Why does this matter for ecology? Because the worst contamination in history came from atmospheric tests — 530 of them — that scattered fallout across oceans and populations that never consented. Japan proposes that this committee declare: any future test explosion must be preceded by a published, independent environmental impact assessment, and that all states reaffirm the moratorium. No more Lucky Dragons. No more silent seas.

**Mod C — "Transboundary monitoring & response" (~150 words):**
> When Fukushima released radioactive water, the ocean did not ask which country owned the current. Within days, monitoring data crossed every border in the Pacific — sometimes faster than governments could share it. Japan knows this from both sides: as the state responsible, and as a victim of fallout that arrived from eighty miles away in 1954. We propose a Transboundary Radiation Monitoring and Early Notification Protocol: real-time data sharing through existing CTBTO and IAEA networks, joint response exercises in the Asia-Pacific first, and open public access to monitoring data. Preparedness is the cheapest disarmament. Japan stands ready to host the first regional exercise. We invite the Pacific islands and all coastal states to co-sponsor.

## 9. DRAFT RESOLUTION SKELETON (Japan-led, co-sponsor: Pacific bloc + Kazakhstan + Nordic)

**Preamble anchors:** recalling the Charter; *recalling* GA resolution on the effects of atomic radiation (UNSCEAR exists); *recalling* NPT (1968) and its 2026 Review Conference outcome; *recalling* CTBT (1996); *recognizing* Stockholm Principle 21 / Rio Principle 2; *recognizing* UNCLOS Part XII and the London Convention/Protocol; *noting* UNSCEAR findings and the 2022 Nature Food climate-famine research; *paying tribute* to the hibakusha and Nihon Hidankyo (2024 Nobel Peace Prize); *deeply concerned* at arsenal modernization and the erosion of the testing moratorium.

**Operative (numbered, each one idea):**
1. Requests the Secretary-General, in cooperation with the IAEA and UNEP, to establish a **Global Radioactive Legacy Remediation and Transparency Fund**, funded by voluntary contributions, to support baseline surveys and remediation of legacy nuclear-test and uranium-mining sites, beginning with pilot projects in the Marshall Islands and Central Asia, subject to host-state consent;
2. Calls upon all states to **reaffirm the moratorium on nuclear test explosions** and to refrain from any action that would undermine it;
3. Requests that **any future nuclear test explosion be preceded by a published independent environmental impact assessment**, conducted in accordance with international standards, with results transmitted to the Secretary-General;
4. Calls upon all states that have not yet done so to **sign and ratify the CTBT**, and welcomes the work of the CTBTO;
5. Urges states parties to the 1986 Conventions on Early Notification and Assistance to implement them fully, and invites other states to accede;
6. Requests the IAEA and CTBTO, in cooperation with member states, to establish an **open transboundary radiation monitoring data portal** and to support regional joint-response exercises, beginning in the Asia-Pacific;
7. Encourages states to **map and report maritime radioactive legacy sites**, including sunken nuclear vessels and historical dumping grounds, consistent with UNCLOS and the London Protocol;
8. Calls for the **NPT review process to adopt an intersessional work programme** on environmental and humanitarian dimensions, building on the 2026 Review Conference;
9. Invites contributions to **capacity-building and technical assistance** for affected developing states, including monitoring equipment and training;
10. Requests the Secretary-General to report to the General Assembly at its next session on implementation, and decides to remain seized of the matter.
