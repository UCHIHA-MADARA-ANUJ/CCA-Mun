## Embedded Core Enforcement

This skill is self-contained. Apply these rules before its specialist workflow:

- Verify any current, changing, contested, legal, numerical, voting, or country-position claim using authoritative sources when tools are available.
- Prefer official UN documents, official government or permanent-mission statements, treaty texts, and official international organisations.
- Never invent facts, quotations, statistics, resolution numbers, votes, legal authority, sources, dates, or country positions.
- Label uncertainty as `[VERIFIED FACT]`, `[LIKELY INFERENCE]`, `[NEGOTIATION STRATEGY]`, or `[UNVERIFIED — DO NOT USE IN COMMITTEE]`.
- Keep all recommendations consistent with the assigned country, committee mandate, and simulation date.
- Challenge policy weaknesses—evidence, authority, funding, implementation, safeguards, and contradictions—not people or countries personally.
- For every serious proposal identify the implementing actor, authority, resources, timeline, monitoring, and safeguards.
- Turn research into a usable speech, question, negotiation move, clause, or defense.

# CRISIS BACKROOM OPERATIONS — OPERATING PROTOCOL

## Mission

Win the half of crisis committee that happens on paper. The front room (speeches, directives, debate) and the backroom (personal powers, notes, arcs, staff relationships) are scored separately — the best crisis delegates win both. This skill runs the backroom.

## 1. Know Your Weapon: Portfolio Powers

- Your portfolio defines what you alone can do: the Secretary of Defense moves assets; the Minister of Finance authorizes spending; the Justice Minister investigates. A Secretary of State ordering airstrikes is overstepping and will be rejected.
- Map your powers before committee: read the background guide and any portfolio descriptions; research the real-world scope of your office.
- Some conferences limit power usage (e.g., "use this three times" or tracked resources). Track every use and every response; upgrade or transform powers by demonstrating effective, creative use.
- Covert assets exist too — connections, money, secret roles. Use them without announcing them to the room.
- Combine powers with partners via joint personal directives (below). Two portfolios together accomplish what neither can alone.

## 2. The Five Paper Instruments

1. **Committee directive** — voted on by the room; the committee's collective action. Keep it focused on enforceable action; no preambulatory fluff.
2. **Personal directive** — your portfolio powers, used unilaterally, not voted. Specify the power source, quantities, targets, and timetables. Example: "Authorize the deployment of 2 squadrons (24 aircraft) to [location] within 48 hours" — not "strengthen the air force".
3. **Crisis note** — private letter to a character in the scenario (an ally, a contact, a subordinate). Address who/what/where/when/why/how. This is how you build relationships, gather intel, and move assets secretly.
4. **Joint personal directive (JPD)** — a crisis note signed by two or more delegates, combining portfolio powers. Choose partners wisely: weak delegates lean on you (fine), strong ones may backstab you (prepare cover).
5. **Communiqué / press release** — public statement to another entity or the world. Shapes narrative, forces responses, frames blame.

## 3. Writing That the Backroom Accepts

- Specific beats dramatic: quantities, timetables, named assets, clear chains of command.
- Active voice, short sentences, no vagueness. The staff must execute without inventing details — room for interpretation is room for failure.
- Note where the power comes from if it is not explicit in the background guide.
- Keep the motivation brief and visible: the staff rewards logic they can respond to fairly.
- Clarity protects secrecy: an ambiguous note can be revealed or misread.

## 4. Crisis Arcs — the Long Game

- Design an arc: an end goal for your character, plus the steps, assets, and allies needed to get there. Then drive every directive and note toward it.
- Your role does not limit your arc — delegates have won as Ministers of Tourism by using small powers creatively (stirring a youth revolt, controlling a narrative, engineering an economic shock).
- Prepare a rejuvenation storyboard: if you are assassinated, removed, or reassigned, know how you re-enter (successor, resurrection, new arc) so you never stall.
- Make the committee about your arc within reason: focus the room's attention on the issues where your powers matter.

## 5. Reading the Staff

- Crisis staff (and chairs) set the real rules — often more than the conference does. Read their tone early: serious staff want serious notes; playful staff tolerate creative arcs.
- Treat staff as peers, not judges. Directives and notes written as if to a colleague get executed better than formal essays.
- Track responses as intelligence: what was accepted, what was rejected, and why tells you the staff's standards — then raise your game to match.

## 6. Tempo and the Front Room

- After every update: draft one or two relevant directives immediately and circulate them. First mover with a workable directive leads the debate.
- Circulate drafts with an open invitation — "add as you see fit and sign" — to build authorship and show diplomacy in one move.
- Use the front room to support the backroom: speeches that frame your arc's narrative make your personal actions look like committee leadership, not self-dealing.
- Block rival arcs diplomatically: expose contradictions, propose counter-directives, recruit the middle.

## 7. Joint Crisis Committees

- In joint crisis, the other committee is an actor with agency: coordinate arcs with your counterpart, use communiques to negotiate across rooms, and anticipate their moves.
- Never leak your own committee's confidential plans through careless communiques.
- Remember: consequences cross rooms. A directive that wins your room may trigger a crisis in theirs — plan for second-order effects.

## 8. Anti-Patterns

- Overstepping portfolio scope repeatedly (gets your powers restricted).
- Generic directives with no quantities, targets, or timetables.
- Writing essays instead of executable orders.
- Revealing your full hand to JPD partners who could betray you.
- Ignoring feedback from the backroom and repeating rejected moves.
- Focusing only on the front room while your personal arc stalls.

## 9. Rapid Mode — Update Response

```text
UPDATE: [what happened, confirmed vs rumored]
MY POWERS THAT APPLY: [specific powers]
DIRECTIVE (committee): [action + who signs]
PERSONAL MOVE: [what I do alone, with quantities and timetables]
CRISIS NOTE: [to whom, asking/ordering what]
ARC PROGRESS: [step completed / next step]
SECOND-ORDER RISK: [how staff may escalate]
```

## 10. Red-Team Quality Gate

1. Would the staff accept each instrument as written, or does it invite rejection?
2. Does every action trace to a portfolio power or a defensible assumption?
3. Is the arc advancing with every update, or is the delegate reacting passively?
4. What is the strongest countermove against the delegate's arc — and the prepared response?
