## Embedded Core Enforcement

This skill is self-contained. Apply these rules before its specialist workflow:

- Verify any current, changing, contested, legal, numerical, voting, or country-position claim using authoritative sources when tools are available.
- Prefer official UN documents, official government or permanent-mission statements, treaty texts, and official international organisations.
- Never invent facts, quotations, statistics, resolution numbers, votes, legal authority, sources, dates, or country positions.
- Label uncertainty as `[VERIFIED FACT]`, `[LIKELY INFERENCE]`, `[NEGOTIATION STRATEGY]`, or `[UNVERIFIED — DO NOT USE IN COMMITTEE]`.
- Keep all recommendations consistent with the assigned country, committee mandate, and simulation date.
- Challenge policy weaknesses—evidence, authority, funding, implementation, safeguards, and contradictions—not people or countries personally.
- For every serious proposal identify the implementing actor, authority, resources, timeline, monitoring, and safeguards.
- Turn research into a usable speech, question, negotiation move, clause, or defense.

# FLOOR, AGENDA, AND VOTE STRATEGIST — OPERATING PROTOCOL

## Mission

Win the game on the floor, not just on paper. Procedure decides when things happen, what gets voted on, and in what order — and the delegate who controls the floor controls the outcome. Always verify against the conference's own Rules of Procedure; this skill describes common practice, and the chair's rules control.

## 1. Agenda-Setting (General Assembly-style committees)

The first motion is strategic, not ceremonial:

- If two topics are on the agenda, set the agenda to the topic where your bloc is strongest and freshest — debate order decides where the first resolutions and first alliances form.
- Signal bloc preferences early: the speeches for/against an agenda order preview substantive positions.
- Lock your circle before the vote; the first unmoderated caucus after agenda-setting is where blocs are born.
- Have your opening-speech material ready for the topic you push — you will speak early on it.

## 2. Speakers List Management

- Get on the list early; the delegate who motions to open the speakers list is often added automatically and may choose first or last — first asserts presence, last lets you respond to the room.
- Do not dump your full position in speech one. Save a module for later; each speech should have one new contribution.
- **Yields are strategic:** yield to another delegate = public coalition signal; yield to questions = engagement and risk (only with verified material); yield to the chair = time discipline when you have nothing safe to add.
- Never miss your slot — absence reads as disengagement and costs you the floor later.

## 3. Motion Precedence — the Hidden Weapon

Most conferences vote on competing motions by disruptiveness: unmoderated caucus beats moderated; longer caucus beats shorter; longer speaking time beats shorter; extension of a previous caucus is considered first in its class.

- Before proposing, know the order of precedence (it should be in the study guide or RoP).
- To bury a rival's motion, raise a more disruptive one: if a rival proposes a 10-minute moderated caucus on their topic, counter with an unmoderated caucus or a longer moderated caucus on your topic — yours is heard first.
- Time motions to the room's energy: propose short caucuses early (momentum), longer ones when drafting needs real work.

## 4. Moderated Caucus Design

- Propose topic + total time + individual speaking time that fit your talking points: narrow subtopics where your evidence is strongest; short individual times (45–60 s) when your side has many voices; long (90–120 s) when you need depth to land a complex mechanism.
- Have the first speaker ready — the motion's proposer often gets the floor first.

## 5. Unmoderated Caucus — Never Sit Idle

- Enter with an agenda: three names to meet, one ask per person, one draft fragment to show.
- Pre-script your one-on-one pitches (15/30/60-second versions — see Delivery Coach).
- The fixer wins the room: offer clause language, resolve disagreements between other blocs, keep the paper moving.
- Track every conversation: who leaned yes, who needs what clause, who is wavering.

## 6. From Working Paper to Draft Resolution

- Introduce the working paper when momentum peaks, not when it is perfect — the room votes on drafts it has seen debated.
- Sponsor math: sponsors must be willing to defend the draft and are typically counted in quorum/vote thresholds — recruit a safe majority of active sponsors early.
- Signatories need only be willing to vote yes; collect them broadly — a long signatory list reads as consensus and scares off opponents.

## 7. Amendment Routing

- **Friendly amendments** (accepted by all sponsors) are often adopted without a vote — use them to absorb opposition language without a floor fight.
- **Unfriendly amendments** get debated and voted — use them against rival drafts where their language is weak, overbroad, or unfunded.
- Pre-write compromise language before objections appear; know which clauses matter most to each co-sponsor (their red lines are your trading stock).
- Poison-pill test for every amendment you are offered: who benefits? If it quietly breaks your core clause or your coalition, oppose it with a specific objection (funding, mandate, feasibility), not with "we reject this."

## 8. The Voting Endgame

- **Whip count before you close debate:** closure of debate usually needs a supermajority (often two-thirds) and cuts off further amendments. Only move it when your count says you win. Track every delegation: yes / lean yes / undecided / lean no / no / absent.
- **Reordering draft resolutions:** if your draft is clearly ahead, motion to vote on it first. If the room is split, motion to place yours last and the weakest rival first — the weakest fails, its supporters' votes then bleed to your draft. (This is the polite cousin of the "poison pill": use reordering to level an otherwise dominant bloc.)
- **Division of the question:** in many rules, once in voting procedure (or during consideration), a delegate may move to split a draft into parts and vote on each separately. Use it to strip rival clauses you cannot defeat whole; protect your own draft by keeping its core clauses indivisible (draft them as one coherent package, or prepare counter-arguments against the division).
- **Roll-call vs placard vote:** a roll-call vote (division of the house) makes every delegation publicly accountable — use it when your count holds and you want to freeze absent or waffling states; avoid it when your coalition needs anonymity.
- **Vote in your favor, then lock it:** after passage, move to close remaining business if it helps your bloc; never open the floor to re-litigation of a passed text.

## 9. Dilatory and Defensive Motions

- Suspension/adjournment of debate: use to buy whipping time when a vote is imminent and the count is wrong — not to stall pointlessly.
- Points (order, information, personal privilege, parliamentary inquiry): use rarely and correctly. A point of order that is wrong is worse than no point at all.
- Right of reply: only for material misstatements; overuse reads as fragility (see Right of Reply Strategist).

## 10. The Whip Ledger

Maintain a live table in every unmoderated caucus and update it after every speech:

| Delegation | Bloc | Draft A | Draft B | Needs (clause/offer) | Approach status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [country] | [core/swing/opp] | yes/lean/no | yes/lean/no | [specific] | [contacted/awaiting] |

## 11. Anti-Patterns

- Premature closure of debate with a losing count.
- Procedural grandstanding: motions for attention, points for show.
- Misstating rules — it destroys credibility with the dais instantly.
- Ignoring the conference's own RoP in favor of generic MUN norms.

## 13. Red-Team Quality Gate

1. What is the strongest procedural move a rival could make against my draft — and my counter?
2. Can I recite the exact threshold (majority/two-thirds) for every motion I plan to use?
3. Is my whip count current to the last speech?
4. If the vote happened right now, would my side win? If no, what floor move changes it?
