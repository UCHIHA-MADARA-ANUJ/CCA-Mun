## 0. EXECUTIVE VERDICT

**FACT:** Eight of the world’s nine nuclear-armed States are represented in this committee: China, France, India, Israel, North Korea, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States. Pakistan is the only nuclear-armed State not listed.

**STRATEGY:** This is probably the committee’s most debate-heavy MOD. Every nuclear possessor has a disclosure record to defend; nearly every non-nuclear State has already supported some form of standardized transparency and accountability.

**Japan’s winning position:**

1. Demand **standardized aggregate reporting**, not operational secrets.
2. Distinguish **stockpile**, **total inventory**, **deployed**, **reserve**, and **retired** warheads.
3. Connect warhead disclosure to **fissile material, dismantlement, radioactive waste and environmental remediation**, or the topic drifts away from the committee agenda.
4. Apply the same rule to every possessor: NPT nuclear-weapon States and non-NPT possessors alike.
5. Admit Japan’s own civilian plutonium issue before China raises it, then use Japan’s annual declarations and IAEA safeguards as the model.

**Main danger:** If Japan demands exact locations, designs, readiness levels or intrusive IAEA warhead inspections, the argument becomes technically wrong and politically impossible.

## 1. DEFINITIONS — DO NOT MIX THESE UP

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Deployed warhead** | A warhead placed on an operational delivery system or located with operational forces, depending on the reporting methodology. |
| **Military stockpile** | Warheads assigned for potential military use, including deployed and reserve/non-deployed warheads. |
| **Retired warhead** | Removed from military stockpile but not yet dismantled. |
| **Total inventory** | Military stockpile plus retired warheads awaiting dismantlement. |
| **Dismantled warhead** | Reduced to component parts; fissile material may still require accounting and disposition. |
| **Fissile material stock** | Highly enriched uranium or separated plutonium in or available for weapons, outside warheads, excess, civilian or safeguarded. |
| **Transparency** | Disclosure that improves understanding and confidence. It may be unilateral, bilateral or multilateral. |
| **Verification** | Procedures that test whether a declaration or commitment is accurate. Transparency is not automatically verification. |

**Critical example:** The United States officially reported **3,748 active and inactive stockpile warheads as of September 2023**. SIPRI estimates a **5,042 total inventory in January 2026** because its total includes retired warheads awaiting dismantlement. Those are different categories and dates—not contradictory figures.

**STRATEGY:** The definitional confusion itself is one of Japan’s strongest arguments for a standard reporting template.

## 3. WHY THIS BELONGS IN AN ECOLOGICAL COMMITTEE

A warhead count is not merely military information. Every nuclear stockpile implies:

- fissile-material production and storage;
- warhead manufacturing and maintenance sites;
- transport and custodial security;
- dismantlement and material disposition;
- contaminated buildings, soil and groundwater;
- long-lived radioactive waste;
- environmental-remediation liabilities.

**FACT:** The US Department of Energy describes its Environmental Management programme as the world’s largest environmental-cleanup programme, created to address five decades of nuclear-weapons production and nuclear-energy research. The legacy includes radioactive waste, excess plutonium and uranium, contaminated facilities, soil and groundwater.

**FACT:** In March 2026, the US Government Accountability Office reported that remaining DOE cleanup work could continue until **2122** and cost **more than half a trillion dollars**. DOE’s fiscal-year 2024 environmental liability was approximately **$544.5 billion**.

**FACT:** DOE’s Legacy Management office oversees long-term surveillance and records for more than 70 former nuclear-weapons production sites.

**STRATEGY:** Japan should prevent the MOD from becoming only “how many bombs?”. The ecological version asks nuclear possessors to disclose aggregate warhead categories **and** the environmental burden of producing, maintaining and dismantling them.

## 4. EXISTING LAW AND POLITICAL COMMITMENTS

### NPT reporting

- The NPT does **not** create a universal verified public register of nuclear warheads.
- The 2010 NPT Action Plan, particularly Actions 5, 20 and 21, supports transparency and reporting by nuclear-weapon States.
- Action 21 encouraged the NPT nuclear-weapon States to agree on a standard reporting form and reporting intervals.
- Japan and the Non-Proliferation and Disarmament Initiative have repeatedly promoted standard reporting templates.

### 2025 cross-regional joint statement

A 9 May 2025 joint statement—supported by many delegations present in this committee—called for:

1. a formalized process for nuclear-weapon-State reports;
2. standardized and comparable information;
3. presentation of reports at NPT meetings;
4. interactive discussions in which questions can be raised;
5. stronger accountability without treating transparency as a substitute for disarmament.

### 2026 NPDI position

The NPDI—Australia, Canada, Chile, Germany, Japan, Mexico, Netherlands, Nigeria, Philippines, Poland, Türkiye and UAE—called for:

- reporting at least twice per NPT Review Cycle;
- standardized formats;
- substantive and comparable information;
- interactive consideration of national reports.

### Legal limits

- The UNGA can recommend standardized voluntary reporting, request Secretary-General/UNODA repositories and convene review processes.
- The UNGA cannot compel classified disclosures or conduct intrusive inspections.
- Ordinary IAEA safeguards do **not** authorize inspections or counting of nuclear warheads.
- CTBTO monitoring detects nuclear explosions; it does **not** count national stockpiles.
- NPT reporting duties apply differently to NPT parties and do not bind India, Israel or North Korea in the same manner. UNGA can invite parallel voluntary reporting by all possessors.

## 5. WHAT CAN SAFELY BE DISCLOSED

### Aggregate categories Japan can defend

1. Total military-stockpile ceiling or aggregate total.
2. Aggregate deployed strategic warheads.
3. Aggregate deployed non-strategic warheads.
4. Reserve/non-deployed warheads.
5. Retired warheads awaiting dismantlement.
6. Annual dismantlement numbers.
7. Fissile material declared excess to military requirements.
8. General nuclear doctrine, no-first-use policy and negative security assurances.
9. Modernization programmes in broad categories.
10. Environmental legacy: aggregate waste, contaminated-site numbers, cleanup funding and timelines.

### Information Japan should explicitly protect

- exact storage locations;
- movement schedules;
- command-and-control details;
- exact readiness cycles;
- weapon designs;
- isotopic/compositional data that could facilitate proliferation;
- vulnerabilities of storage or delivery systems.

**STRATEGY:** A tiered disclosure model—aggregate public data, protected technical exchanges and negotiated verification—defeats the “national security” objection without demanding operational secrets.

## 6. CURRENT TRANSPARENCY RECORD OF THE EIGHT NUCLEAR-ARMED DELEGATIONS

### United States — qualified transparency leader

**FACT:** NNSA officially disclosed 3,748 active/inactive stockpile warheads as of September 2023, an 88 per cent reduction from the 1967 peak. It also reported 12,088 dismantlements during fiscal years 1994–2023.

**Likely argument:** The US is already more transparent than most nuclear States; reporting must be reciprocal and protect national security.

**Japan’s leverage:** Praise US disclosure first, then call for a current annual standardized report and ask every possessor to match it.

### Russia — principal transparency collapse

**FACT:** Russia suspended New START participation in 2023, ending inspections and data exchanges. New START expired 5 February 2026. A senior US official stated there was no informal continuing agreement.

**Likely argument:** Transparency is impossible without strategic stability and must include US, UK and French forces.

**Japan’s leverage:** Agree that every possessor—including UK and France—must be included. Do not let Russia use NATO as an excuse for zero reporting.

### China — intention transparency, quantitative opacity

**FACT:** China’s 2025 white paper reaffirms no first use, self-defence and minimum deterrence. It argues transparency must be voluntary, tailored to national conditions and focused on intentions and policies. China has not published an official warhead count.

**Likely argument:** The vastly larger US/Russian arsenals must reduce first; no-first-use is the most meaningful transparency measure.

**Japan’s leverage:** Welcome the no-first-use statement, then state that policy transparency is valuable but cannot substitute for aggregate, comparable information.

### France — dramatic 2026 reversal

**FACT:** France historically disclosed a stockpile below 300 and details of force composition. On 2 March 2026, President Macron announced an increase in warheads and stated France would no longer release arsenal figures.

**Likely argument:** France has a strong past record; current conditions require strategic ambiguity and “strict sufficiency.”

**Japan’s leverage:** France previously demonstrated that aggregate counts coexist with deterrence. Its reversal is the MOD’s clearest accountability test.

### United Kingdom — ceiling without operational detail

**FACT:** The UK publishes an overall ceiling of no more than 260 warheads but withholds operational-stockpile, deployed-warhead and deployed-missile numbers. Its 2026 NPT national report defends limits to transparency on national-security grounds.

**Likely argument:** The ceiling and national report are sufficient; more detail could harm deterrence and proliferation security.

**Japan’s leverage:** A ceiling is useful but not comparable reporting. Japan should ask for categories without exact locations or readiness information.

### India — public doctrine, unpublished count

**FACT:** India’s public doctrine includes credible minimum deterrence and no first use; India has not published an official warhead number. SIPRI estimates approximately 190.

**Likely argument:** Larger arsenals must lead; minimum deterrence is not a numerical competition; disclosures must be universal and non-discriminatory.

**Japan’s leverage:** Agree on special US/Russian responsibility but ask India to translate “minimum” into standardized aggregate accountability. Protect India from demands for location-level disclosure.

### Israel — deliberate nuclear ambiguity

**FACT:** Israel is outside the NPT and neither confirms nor denies nuclear possession. External estimates place its arsenal near 90, but there is no official count. Israel argues ambiguity serves regional deterrence.

**Likely argument:** Disclosure is impossible without regional peace and would destabilize deterrence.

**Japan’s leverage:** Opacity externalizes uncertainty onto every neighbouring State. Aggregate voluntary reporting need not disclose doctrine, locations or targeting plans.

### North Korea — extreme opacity

**FACT:** North Korea has tested nuclear weapons and declared nuclear status, but does not publish an authoritative arsenal count. Estimates vary substantially; SIPRI estimates around 60 as of January 2026.

**Likely argument:** Its arsenal is sovereign and defensive; hostile policy must end first.

**Japan’s leverage:** A self-declared nuclear State cannot invoke recognition and deny the most basic aggregate accountability simultaneously.

## 7. JAPAN’S OFFICIAL POSITION AND COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE

### Official policy basis

- Japan’s Hiroshima Action Plan names enhancing transparency as one of its five central actions.
- Japan has proposed standardized nuclear-force reporting within the NPT review process.
- Japan is a founding member of the NPDI and supports standardized, comparable reports and interactive review.
- Japan’s 2010 NPT statement praised US stockpile disclosure and called on other possessors to follow suit.

### Japan’s strongest line of policy

Japan supports transparency that is:

- regular;
- standardized;
- comparable;
- aggregate;
- reciprocal;
- compatible with national-security and non-proliferation protections;
- linked to environmental and fissile-material accountability.

### Japan’s plutonium vulnerability

**FACT:** Japan officially reported 44.4 tonnes of separated civilian plutonium as of the end of 2024: 8.6 tonnes in Japan and 35.8 tonnes held abroad. Japan has published detailed annual data since 1994 and submits information to the IAEA under the Guidelines for the Management of Plutonium.

**FACT:** Japan’s official report states that all Japanese nuclear material is subject to comprehensive IAEA safeguards and the Additional Protocol. The IAEA reached the broader conclusion that all nuclear material remained in peaceful activities for 2024.

**FACT:** Japan’s estimated stockpile remained approximately 44.4 tonnes at the end of fiscal year 2025; the JAEC projects a decline from use of MOX fuel.

**Critical distinction:** Civil separated plutonium under safeguards is not a nuclear-weapons stockpile. The IAEA “significant quantity” benchmark is a safeguards-detection goal—not a claim that dividing tonnes by eight kilograms equals deliverable warheads.

### Expected Chinese attack

China may cite Japan’s 44.4 tonnes and allege “nuclear latency.”

### Japan’s defence

1. Acknowledge the figure immediately; hiding it proves the opponent’s point.
2. Distinguish civilian fissile material from weapon stockpiles.
3. Cite annual public reporting, IAEA safeguards and the broader conclusion.
4. State Japan’s policy of reducing holdings and not possessing plutonium without specified peaceful purposes.
5. Redirect: Japan asks nuclear possessors to publish equivalent aggregate information on their warheads and weapon-grade fissile materials.

## 10. OPPOSING ARGUMENTS — THE BEST VERSION OF EACH

### “Disclosure compromises deterrence.”

Strongest version: Aggregate numbers can still reveal force trends and vulnerabilities, particularly for smaller arsenals.

Japan’s answer: Use categories and totals, not locations, movement schedules, designs or readiness details. US, UK and former French practice proves some quantitative disclosure can coexist with deterrence.

### “Larger arsenals must go first.”

Strongest version: The US and Russia hold 86 per cent of global weapons and carry special responsibility.

Japan’s answer: Agree on special responsibility, but a universal standardized framework prevents smaller possessors from remaining permanently opaque.

### “No first use is sufficient transparency.”

Strongest version: Intentions and doctrine matter more for risk reduction than exact numbers.

Japan’s answer: Policy transparency is valuable but intentions can change. Comparable aggregate categories are needed to assess implementation and trends.

### “Transparency is not disarmament.”

Strongest version: Reporting can become a substitute for actual reductions.

Japan’s answer: Agree. The 2025 joint statement says transparency is not a substitute; it is a necessary basis for accountability and verification.

### “Non-NPT possessors have no reporting obligation.”

Strongest version: NPT mechanisms cannot bind India, Israel or North Korea.

Japan’s answer: UNGA can invite voluntary parallel reporting under a universal confidence-building framework without inventing treaty obligations.

### “IAEA can inspect and verify all warheads.”

This is technically wrong. Ordinary safeguards do not cover military warheads. Special negotiated mandates, information barriers and dedicated verification arrangements would be required.

## 13. POLICY ARCHITECTURE FOR LATER USE — NOT YET A SPEECH

### Disclosure ladder

**Tier 1 — Public aggregate report**

- military stockpile;
- deployed strategic/non-strategic categories;
- reserve/non-deployed;
- retired awaiting dismantlement;
- annual dismantlements;
- doctrine and security assurances;
- excess military fissile material;
- aggregate environmental-cleanup burden.

**Tier 2 — Protected interstate exchanges**

- more detailed type/category data;
- notifications;
- delivery-system categories;
- protected technical discussions.

**Tier 3 — Negotiated verification**

- information barriers;
- chain of custody;
- authenticated radiation measurements;
- inspections only by negotiated consent and mandate.

### Institutional path

1. Standardized voluntary NPT template, submitted at least twice per Review Cycle.
2. Interactive NPT sessions where nuclear-weapon States answer questions.
3. UNODA repository for public aggregate reports.
4. Parallel voluntary UNGA reporting track for non-NPT possessors.
5. Environmental annex covering waste, contaminated sites, declared excess material, cleanup funding and timelines.
6. Independent civil-society index as a supplement—not a substitute for official reporting.

## 14. SAFE FACTS VS UNSAFE CLAIMS

### Safe

- “SIPRI estimates 12,187 warheads globally as of January 2026.”
- “The US officially disclosed 3,748 stockpile warheads as of September 2023.”
- “The UK publishes a ceiling of no more than 260 but withholds operational numbers.”
- “France announced in March 2026 that it would increase its arsenal and stop publishing figures.”
- “China publishes no official warhead number and emphasizes no first use and minimum deterrence.”
- “New START expired 5 February 2026; there is no legally binding successor.”
- “Japan reports 44.4 tonnes of separated civilian plutonium under IAEA safeguards.”

### Unsafe / forbidden

- “China officially has 620 warheads.” — SIPRI estimate, not official.
- “The US has exactly 5,042 stockpiled warheads.” — SIPRI total inventory; official stockpile category is different.
- “Iran has nuclear weapons.” — unsupported and contrary to the known official record.
- “Japan has enough plutonium for 5,500 warheads.” — misuses the IAEA significant-quantity benchmark.
- “The IAEA can inspect every warhead.” — false under ordinary safeguards.
- “Transparency requires publishing exact storage locations.” — strategically unnecessary and dangerous.
- “France currently has exactly 290.” — external estimate; France stopped numerical disclosure in March 2026.
- “North Korea has exactly 60.” — estimate with substantial uncertainty.
