## Delegation of Japan · CCA MUN ’26 · UNGA

**Original selected topic:** “Deliberation on existing loopholes in the nuclear treaties”  
**Speaking time:** 75 seconds  
**Status:** Research, attack map and solution architecture only — **NO SPEECH DRAFTED**  
**Research position:** Use the whole nuclear-governance regime, but do not call every deliberate scope limit a “loophole.”

---

## 0. EXECUTIVE VERDICT

The topic is broad enough to produce repetitive speeches unless Japan does something the room will not expect:

> **Do not list treaties. Classify the gaps.**

Japan should divide the regime’s weaknesses into six legally distinct categories:

1. **Universality gap** — important States remain outside a treaty or protocol.
2. **Scope gap** — an activity or material was deliberately excluded.
3. **Verification gap** — an obligation exists but the inspection or reporting machinery is absent, incomplete or inactive.
4. **Implementation gap** — States have commitments but no timetable, common metric or enforcement pathway.
5. **Withdrawal / expiry gap** — a State can leave, suspend implementation or allow a treaty to expire without a successor.
6. **Environmental-remediation gap** — treaties regulate acquisition, testing or safeguards but leave contamination, military waste, victim assistance and civil liability elsewhere.

### Japan’s winning thesis

The deepest loophole is not a hidden comma. It is **fragmentation**:

- the NPT checks proliferation but does not create a weapons-environment cleanup code;
- IAEA safeguards verify non-diversion, not ecological safety;
- the CTBT bans explosions but has not entered into force and cannot yet conduct treaty on-site inspections;
- the TPNW contains victim-assistance and remediation duties, but no nuclear-armed State is party;
- civil nuclear-liability conventions focus on peaceful installations and do not form a universal regime for weapons legacies;
- the Joint Convention normally excludes military or defence waste unless a State declares it or transfers it permanently to civilian management.

**High-value conclusion:** A State can satisfy one treaty and still leave a major ecological risk outside that treaty’s scope.

## 1. TERMINOLOGY FIREWALL

### Correct usage

- **NPT Article III:** IAEA safeguards for non-nuclear-weapon States.
- **NPT Article IV:** peaceful-use rights and cooperation.
- **NPT Article VI:** good-faith negotiations on disarmament and cessation of the nuclear arms race.
- **NPT Article X:** withdrawal after three months’ notice where extraordinary events are said to jeopardize supreme interests.

### Never say

- “Article IV safeguards.” — factually wrong.
- “The NPT requires every nuclear-weapon State to submit all military facilities to safeguards.” — false.
- “The Additional Protocol is mandatory under the NPT.” — false; it is a separate safeguards instrument.
- “The CTBT is legally in force.” — false.
- “CTBTO can order an on-site inspection today.” — false before entry into force.
- “The TPNW binds the nuclear-armed States.” — none is currently a party.
- “New START has a loophole.” — it expired; that is now a regulatory vacuum, not a live-treaty loophole.
- “All military nuclear waste is covered by the Joint Convention.” — false unless declared or permanently transferred into exclusively civilian programmes.

### Strong correction line if another delegate is wrong

> **“The delegation is describing Article III safeguards as Article IV. Article IV protects peaceful use. Japan asks the committee to correct the law before proposing to reform it.”**

Use this once. It will immediately establish legal command.

# 2. THE TREATY-GAP MATRIX

## A. NPT — the central bargain with structural asymmetries

### Gap 1: Non-universality

India, Israel and Pakistan never joined. DPRK announced withdrawal. The treaty’s verification and review machinery therefore does not bind all possessors identically.

**Aggressive question:**

> Can a regime be called universal when several nuclear-armed States remain outside it and one withdrew after acquiring nuclear capacity?

**Legal precision:** Do not say DPRK’s withdrawal status is wholly uncontested; debates remain over procedure and consequences. It is enough to state that DPRK announced withdrawal and is not treated as an active NPT party.

### Gap 2: The 1967 recognition cutoff

Article IX recognizes as nuclear-weapon States only States that manufactured and exploded a nuclear weapon before 1 January 1967. This created the treaty’s foundational asymmetry: five recognized NWS and all later possessors outside that status.

**Distinct point:** This is not a drafting accident. It was the political price of creating the treaty.

### Gap 3: Article VI has no numerical deadline

Article VI requires good-faith negotiations, but the treaty contains no numerical elimination deadline, common reduction schedule or automatic consequence for stalled progress.

**Safe claim:** “No treaty deadline or enforcement schedule.”  
**Unsafe claim:** “Article VI has no legal force.”

### Gap 4: Article X withdrawal

A party may withdraw on three months’ notice after invoking extraordinary events affecting supreme interests. The treaty itself does not empower other parties to veto withdrawal.

Key unresolved policy questions:

- What happens to material, facilities and technology acquired while the State was a party?
- Should safeguards survive withdrawal under supply agreements?
- What consequences follow if violations occurred before withdrawal?
- Must the Security Council automatically meet?

### Gap 5: Safeguards asymmetry

Article III requires comprehensive safeguards for NNWS parties’ peaceful nuclear material. It does not impose equivalent comprehensive IAEA safeguards on NWS military programmes.

**Ecological significance:** Safeguards answer diversion questions. They do not automatically disclose warheads, military waste, contaminated production facilities or remediation liabilities.

### Gap 6: Additional Protocol is not universal

The AP strengthens IAEA tools for detecting undeclared material and activity, but is not automatically mandatory under the NPT. States have different safeguards coverage.

### Gap 7: Naval-propulsion / non-proscribed military use

Paragraph 14 of the model Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement, INFCIRC/153, provides for non-application of the agreement’s normal safeguards procedures to nuclear material used in non-proscribed military activity, generally discussed in relation to naval propulsion, under a special arrangement with the IAEA.

**Do not oversimplify:**

- the NPT prohibits nuclear explosive uses, not every military use;
- paragraph 14 is not a free right to “remove material and disappear”;
- the State must notify the IAEA and conclude an arrangement;
- the beginning, duration, continuity of knowledge and return to safeguards are the core verification concerns.

**Winning question:**

> What uniform minimum verification measures must apply when normal safeguards are not applied to naval fuel, while still protecting classified propulsion information?

### Gap 8: Nuclear sharing is legally contested

NATO maintains that nuclear sharing is NPT-consistent because weapons remain under US control in peacetime and the treaty would no longer govern after war began. Critics argue the arrangements undermine Articles I and II.

**Safety rule:** Call it a “contested interpretation,” never an adjudicated violation.

### Gap 9: Environmental silence

The NPT contains no dedicated code for:

- weapons-production contamination;
- military radioactive waste;
- testing-site remediation;
- victim assistance;
- environmental liability;
- cross-border legacy archives.

This is Japan’s most agenda-relevant point.

## B. IAEA SAFEGUARDS — indispensable but frequently misdescribed

Safeguards are agreements and verification machinery linked to the NPT, not an all-purpose environmental regulator.

### What they do

- verify that safeguarded nuclear material is not diverted;
- support conclusions regarding declared and, under the AP, undeclared activities;
- inspect according to the applicable agreement.

### What they do not automatically do

- count every military warhead;
- inspect all NWS military sites;
- determine environmental liability;
- remediate contaminated weapons sites;
- compensate victims.

### Strong Japan point

> **A State can be in safeguards compliance and still have an environmental problem. Conversely, an environmental incident does not itself prove weapons diversion.**

This distinction protects Japan’s peaceful civilian programme while preserving ecological accountability.

---

## C. PARTIAL TEST BAN TREATY, 1963 — the historic underground gap

The treaty prohibits tests in the atmosphere, outer space and underwater. It also prohibits an explosion in another environment where radioactive debris is present outside the testing State’s territorial limits.

It did **not** comprehensively prohibit contained underground tests. Testing moved underground.

### Aggressive point

> The treaty reduced atmospheric fallout but converted a global prohibition problem into a containment and verification problem.

### Do not say

- “It freely permits any underground test.” The cross-border radioactive-debris condition still matters.
- “France and China violated it by atmospheric tests.” They did not sign the treaty.

---

## D. CTBT — a near-universal norm without entry into force

### Current status

- 188 signatures;
- 179 ratifications;
- entry into force still requires nine Annex 2 States: China, Egypt, India, Iran, Israel, DPRK, Pakistan, Russia and the United States;
- India, DPRK and Pakistan have not signed;
- Russia revoked ratification but remains a signatory.

### Gap 1: Entry-into-force lock

The Article XIV formula means the treaty remains outside legal force until every required Annex 2 State ratifies.

### Gap 2: On-site inspections unavailable

The IMS operates and distributes data, but formal treaty on-site inspections are available only after entry into force.

### Gap 3: Zero-yield and non-explosive experimentation

The CTBT prohibits any nuclear weapon test explosion or other nuclear explosion. It does not prohibit computer simulation, hydrodynamic work or genuinely subcritical experiments that do not produce a self-sustaining supercritical nuclear yield.

**Be careful:** States dispute compliance and technical interpretations. Do not accuse a country of a covert test unless clearly attributing the allegation and the denial/uncertainty.

### Gap 4: No remediation mandate

The CTBT detects and deters explosions. It does not create a universal system for remediating damage from tests conducted before the treaty.

---

## E. TPNW — strongest humanitarian obligations, weakest possessor participation

### Strengths

- categorical prohibitions for parties;
- Articles 6 and 7 on victim assistance, environmental remediation and international cooperation;
- Article 7(6) gives a special assistance responsibility to a party that used or tested nuclear weapons.

### Gaps

1. No nuclear-armed State is a party.
2. Most source/testing powers are therefore not bound by Articles 6 and 7.
3. Obligations focus on areas under a party’s jurisdiction or control and assistance among parties.
4. The treaty does not itself create a universal compensation court.
5. Implementation and reporting mechanisms for positive obligations continue to be developed through Meetings of States Parties.

### Aggressive point

> The treaty with the strongest remediation provisions binds almost none of the States that created the largest testing legacies.

### Bridge for Japan

Japan can support using Articles 6 and 7 as a policy model without falsely claiming Japan is a party.

---

## F. NEW START AND COLLAPSED ARMS CONTROL — regulatory vacuum, not “loophole”

New START expired on 5 February 2026. Inspections and data exchanges had already ceased after Russia suspended participation in 2023.

### Coverage limits while it operated

- bilateral US–Russia only;
- strategic deployed forces and launchers;
- did not comprehensively cover reserve, retired or non-strategic warheads;
- did not include China, France, UK, India, Pakistan, Israel or DPRK;
- did not regulate environmental liabilities.

### Current reality

No legally binding successor means the two largest arsenals now lack treaty limits and reciprocal inspection under New START.

### USA-friendly use

Praise the value of the treaty’s prior verification architecture; do not frame the United States as the sole cause of expiry. Demand a successor and interim aggregate reporting from both largest arsenals.

---

## G. FISSILE MATERIAL CUTOFF — the missing treaty

No universal FMCT is in force.

Main dispute:

- whether a treaty should prohibit only future production;
- whether existing fissile-material stocks should be included;
- verification scope;
- linkage to regional security.

**Precision:** This is an absent instrument, not a loophole inside an existing treaty.

---

## H. JOINT CONVENTION ON SPENT FUEL AND RADIOACTIVE WASTE — military exclusion

Article 3 primarily covers spent fuel from civilian reactors and radioactive waste from civilian applications.

Military or defence spent fuel and waste are outside the Convention unless:

- the State declares them for Convention purposes; or
- they are transferred permanently to and managed within exclusively civilian programmes.

### Aggressive ecological point

> The principal global waste-safety convention allows the material most closely associated with national security to remain outside routine peer reporting until the State brings it into civilian management.

### Constructive solution

Invite voluntary military-waste environmental annexes using protected aggregate categories, without demanding operational locations.

---

## I. EARLY NOTIFICATION AND ASSISTANCE CONVENTIONS — accident coverage does not equal weapons coverage

The Early Notification Convention requires rapid notification for covered accidents involving listed facilities or activities where a transboundary release may be radiologically significant. States may also notify other accidents voluntarily.

### Gap

It is not a universal automatic notification code for every nuclear-weapon accident, military activity, historical release or contamination discovery.

### Japan point

Use the Convention as a **design model**, not as proof that every military event is already legally covered.

---

## J. CIVIL NUCLEAR-LIABILITY CONVENTIONS — fragmented and peace-oriented

The Vienna/Paris/CSC system generally channels strict liability to the operator of a peaceful nuclear installation, limits jurisdiction and requires financial security.

Main gaps:

- uneven global participation;
- different treaty versions and national limits;
- installations used for non-peaceful purposes excluded from the CSC framework;
- armed-conflict/hostilities exemptions in the liability regime;
- not designed as a compensation system for historical nuclear testing or weapons-production legacies;
- geographic and reciprocity rules can complicate transboundary claims.

### Aggressive point

> The international regime most developed for paying nuclear damage was designed around civilian operators—not the State-controlled military activities most central to this agenda.

---

## K. NUCLEAR-WEAPON-FREE ZONES — protocol and transit gaps

Regional treaties strengthen non-proliferation, but their effectiveness depends on nuclear-weapon-State protocols and agreed territorial scope.

### Southeast Asia example

No NWS has signed or ratified the Bangkok Treaty Protocol. Objections concern:

- EEZ and continental-shelf scope;
- freedom of navigation;
- transit and port calls;
- negative security assurances.

### Distinct committee positions

- Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore can demand protocol movement;
- USA, France, UK, Russia and China must address geographic and transit concerns;
- Japan can broker clarification without demanding disclosure of ship payloads.

---

## L. OUTER SPACE, SEABED AND ANTARCTIC TREATIES — geographic prohibitions, not total disarmament

These instruments prohibit nuclear or WMD activities in defined locations:

- Outer Space Treaty: no WMD in orbit or stationed on celestial bodies; does not prohibit ballistic missiles merely travelling through space.
- Seabed Treaty: no emplacement of nuclear weapons on the seabed beyond the treaty’s coastal limit; does not prohibit nuclear-armed submarines moving through the water column.
- Antarctic Treaty: prohibits nuclear explosions and radioactive-waste disposal in Antarctica.

**Winning point:** Geographic arms-control law is a patchwork. Prohibited placement in one domain does not equal prohibition of possession, transit or environmental externality everywhere.

## M. OTHER INSTRUMENTS — rapid gap map

| Instrument | What it addresses | What remains outside / limited |
|---|---|---|
| Threshold Test Ban Treaty (1974) | Bilateral US–Soviet/Russian ceiling of 150 kilotons for underground weapon tests | Bilateral only; tests below threshold remained possible; later superseded normatively by CTBT ambition |
| Peaceful Nuclear Explosions Treaty (1976) | Bilateral limits and verification for so-called peaceful nuclear explosions | Did not create a universal prohibition or remediation code |
| Convention on Nuclear Safety | Safety of land-based civil nuclear power plants | Not a military reactor, weapons facility or testing-legacy convention |
| CPPNM + 2005 Amendment | Physical protection of nuclear material/facilities used for peaceful purposes and prevention of theft/sabotage | Does not create nuclear disarmament, testing-remediation or military-stockpile transparency duties |
| Nuclear Terrorism Convention | Criminalization/cooperation against terrorist use of radioactive or nuclear material | Non-State criminal threat framework, not State arsenal regulation or historic remediation |
| ENMOD Convention | Prohibits military or hostile environmental-modification techniques with widespread, long-lasting or severe effects | Does not generally regulate all environmental damage caused by nuclear weapons or production |
| UNSCR 1540 | Binding Security Council duties to prevent non-State actors obtaining WMD-related materials | Not a disarmament treaty; no victim-assistance or source-State remediation system |
| IAEA Additional Protocol | Expanded safeguards information/access to help detect undeclared activity | Not universal and not a weapons-environment treaty |
| Small Quantities Protocols | Reduced safeguards procedures for States with little or no nuclear material | Revised model strengthened the system, but implementation status still varies |

### Classification rule

Do not call UNSCR 1540 or IAEA safeguards documents “nuclear treaties.” Refer to the wider **nuclear-governance architecture**.

# 3. THE FIVE BEST NON-GENERIC SPEAKING LANES

These are research lanes, not speech text.

## Lane 1 — “Safeguarded” does not mean “environmentally harmless”

- Article III = non-diversion.
- Article IV = peaceful-use right.
- Environmental notification/remediation = separate gap.
- Protects Japan while correcting the room.

## Lane 2 — Military waste sits outside the strongest global waste convention

- Joint Convention Article 3(3).
- This is obscure, factual and directly ecological.
- Propose protected aggregate military-waste annexes.

## Lane 3 — The test ban cannot inspect because it is not in force

- 179 ratifications but nine Annex 2 ratifications missing.
- IMS exists; OSI unavailable.
- Propose entry into force + interim confidence-building visits/data.

## Lane 4 — Victim/remediation law binds almost no source States

- TPNW Articles 6/7 strong.
- No nuclear-armed State party.
- Propose UNGA parallel voluntary implementation.

## Lane 5 — The liability regime excludes the central military problem

- Civil operator-centred system.
- Military/non-peaceful facilities excluded or outside normal scope.
- Historical testing not its design.
- Propose assistance without prejudging civil or State liability.

### Recommended 75-second choice

Use **Lane 2 + Lane 4**, with Lane 1 as the opening correction. Few delegates will discover the Joint Convention military exclusion.

# 5. AGGRESSIVE ATTACK–DEFENCE BANK

## “Treaties are already sufficient; implementation is the problem.”

**Counter:** Some gaps are implementation failures, but Article 3(3) of the Joint Convention is an express scope exclusion and CTBT on-site inspection is legally unavailable before entry into force. Those are structural, not rhetorical.

## “Naval propulsion is not a loophole.”

**Counter:** Do not argue labels. Ask which verification measures preserve continuity of knowledge when normal safeguards procedures do not apply. The technical question survives the semantic defence.

## “The TPNW solves remediation.”

**Counter:** It creates strong duties for parties, but no nuclear-armed State is party. Ask how affected non-parties and source non-parties enter the machinery.

## “Civil liability already compensates nuclear harm.”

**Counter:** Civil liability is operator-centred and peace-installation focused; it was not designed to remedy historical weapons tests or all military nuclear damage.

## “NPT Article IV prevents these environmental restrictions.”

**Counter:** Article IV protects peaceful use in conformity with Articles I and II. A neutral environmental-notification rule is not a weapons allegation and need not restrict peaceful technology.

## “Japan is weaponizing safeguards against developing States.”

**Counter:** Japan is demanding different rules for different legal questions: safeguards for diversion, environmental assessment for contamination, and protected reporting for military waste.

# 7. SAFE CLAIMS / QUARANTINED CLAIMS

## Safe

- NPT Article III concerns safeguards; Article IV concerns peaceful use.
- NPT Article X permits withdrawal after three months’ notice under its conditions.
- CTBT is not in force; treaty on-site inspections are unavailable.
- Nine Annex 2 ratifications remain outstanding as of 2026.
- Joint Convention Article 3(3) normally excludes military/defence waste unless declared or permanently transferred to civilian management.
- TPNW Articles 6/7 address victims and remediation; no nuclear-armed State is party.
- New START expired on 5 February 2026.
- Civil nuclear-liability conventions do not form a universal weapons-legacy compensation regime.

## Quarantine

- “Nuclear sharing is definitely illegal.” — legally contested.
- “AUKUS removes all naval fuel permanently from any IAEA oversight.” — arrangement still requires IAEA engagement; do not prejudge details.
- “Subcritical experiments violate CTBT.” — not if genuinely non-explosive under the zero-yield interpretation.
- “The NPT withdrawal clause lets States keep imported material automatically.” — supply agreements and Security Council action may matter.
- “All military waste is unregulated.” — domestic laws may regulate it; the point is the international convention’s scope.

## FINAL RESEARCH CONCLUSION

Japan should not claim it can close every nuclear-treaty loophole. It should establish the rule the room missed:

> **Where one treaty stops, the risk does not. The committee must identify which legal instrument governs the next question—or admit that no instrument currently does.**

That is the difference between treaty recital and treaty analysis.
