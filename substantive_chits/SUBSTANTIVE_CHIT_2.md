# SUBSTANTIVE CHIT 02 — THE ARSENAL LEDGER

**Delegation:** Japan

**Committee:** United Nations General Assembly

**Conference:** CCA MUN ’26

**Agenda:** Assessing the ecological threats of nuclear proliferation on global boundaries

**MOD 2 focus:** Transparency and accountability in nuclear-weapons stockpile disclosure

---

## Submission

Stockpile opacity is not only a disarmament problem. It conceals the scale of fissile-material production, weapons maintenance, transport, retirement, dismantlement, radioactive waste and long-term environmental remediation. Japan therefore proposes a **Standardized Nuclear Forces and Environmental Accountability Framework** built on three disciplines: **REPORT, PROTECT and ACCOUNT**.

## I. The present record is neither complete nor comparable

SIPRI estimates that nine nuclear-armed States possessed approximately **12,187 nuclear weapons** in January 2026: **9,745** in military stockpiles and approximately **4,012 deployed** with operational forces.[1] These are estimates, not official national declarations. SIPRI also warns that reduced transparency increasingly obstructs assessment.

The first problem is definitional. A **military stockpile** contains warheads assigned for possible military use, including deployed and reserve weapons. A **total inventory** adds retired warheads awaiting dismantlement. A **dismantled warhead** is no longer a warhead, although its fissile material still requires accounting and disposition.

The United States officially reported **3,748 active and inactive stockpile warheads as of September 2023**, while SIPRI estimates a larger **total inventory** for January 2026 because that category includes retired warheads awaiting dismantlement. These figures are not contradictory; they answer different questions. The United States also reported **12,088 dismantlements** during fiscal years 1994–2023.[2]

Disclosure practices remain uneven. The United Kingdom publishes a ceiling of no more than **260 warheads** but withholds its operational-stockpile, deployed-warhead and deployed-missile figures.[3] France previously demonstrated that numerical transparency and deterrence could coexist, but on 2 March 2026 announced an arsenal increase and stated that it would no longer release arsenal figures.[4] China publishes doctrine—including no first use and minimum deterrence—but no official warhead total.[5] New START expired on 5 February 2026 without a legally binding successor, eliminating the last binding limits on the two largest strategic arsenals.[6]

## II. The ecological information hidden behind the count

Every stockpile implies a material lifecycle: fissile-material production and storage, warhead manufacture and maintenance, transport, retirement, dismantlement, disposition, waste and contaminated facilities. The United States Government Accountability Office reports that Department of Energy sites remain contaminated from decades of weapons production and nuclear-energy research. In May 2025, the Department estimated remaining Environmental Management cleanup above **half a trillion dollars**.[7]

A stockpile report that excludes its environmental liabilities is therefore incomplete. A warhead may leave the military stockpile years before its components, waste and contaminated site leave the public balance sheet.

## III. Existing authority and the remaining gap

The 2010 NPT Action Plan encouraged nuclear-weapon States to agree on a standard reporting form and appropriate reporting intervals without prejudice to national security.[8] A cross-regional statement delivered on 9 May 2025 called for standardized and comparable national reports and interactive discussion.[9] In 2026, the NPDI called for nuclear-weapon States to report at least twice per Review Cycle, using standardized formats and dedicated interactive consideration.[10]

Yet no universal official warhead register exists. Ordinary IAEA safeguards do not authorize counting military warheads, and CTBTO monitoring detects nuclear explosions rather than national stockpile size. The General Assembly can recommend voluntary reporting and establish a public repository; it cannot compel classified operational disclosure. Japan’s framework is built within those limits.

## IV. Japan’s three-part framework

### 1. REPORT — One vocabulary, comparable categories

A voluntary public report should state, where applicable:

- total military stockpile or declared ceiling;
- aggregate deployed strategic and non-strategic categories;
- reserve or non-deployed warheads;
- retired warheads awaiting dismantlement;
- annual dismantlement numbers;
- fissile material declared excess to military requirements;
- broad doctrine, risk-reduction measures and modernization categories;
- an **Environmental Accountability Annex** covering former production sites, waste categories, contaminated soil or groundwater, cleanup expenditure and projected timelines.

Every figure must carry a date, definition, source and uncertainty note. National data and external estimates must never be presented as equivalent.

### 2. PROTECT — Transparency without an operational blueprint

Japan proposes three information tiers:

- **Tier I — Public aggregates:** totals, ceilings, categories, dismantlement and environmental liabilities;
- **Tier II — Protected exchanges:** more detailed type and notification data shared only through agreed channels;
- **Tier III — Negotiated verification:** managed access, information barriers and inspection only under a specific mandate and consent.

Public reports must exclude exact storage locations, movement schedules, command-and-control arrangements, precise readiness cycles, weapon designs, isotopic details that facilitate proliferation and exploitable vulnerabilities.

### 3. ACCOUNT — Reports must be questioned and connected to cleanup

Reports should be deposited in a public UNODA repository and considered in dedicated interactive sessions. NPT nuclear-weapon States should report at least twice per Review Cycle; non-NPT possessors should be invited into a parallel voluntary UN track using the same public categories. States should answer technical questions, correct inconsistent definitions and update retired or dismantled figures.

The Environmental Accountability Annex should be reviewed beside the force report so committee members can trace the lifecycle from stockpile to dismantlement, waste and remediation. Transparency is not a substitute for disarmament; it is the baseline that makes progress measurable.

## V. Evidence-to-action control logic

- **Incompatible categories** create false contradictions; **REPORT** supplies common definitions, dates and uncertainty labels.
- **Legitimate security concerns** are used to defend total opacity; **PROTECT** separates safe aggregates from operational secrets.
- **Reports deposited without scrutiny** create transparency theatre; **ACCOUNT** requires interactive questions and corrections.
- **Warhead counts separated from environmental costs** hide the material lifecycle; the **Environmental Annex** connects arsenal size to waste and remediation.

## VI. Draft-ready committee recommendations

1. **Requests** the Secretary-General, through UNODA and in consultation with interested States, to develop a non-binding Standardized Nuclear Forces and Environmental Accountability Template and public repository;
2. **Encourages** NPT nuclear-weapon States to report at least twice per Review Cycle and **invites** non-NPT possessors to submit parallel voluntary reports using comparable public categories, followed by dedicated interactive consideration;
3. **Calls upon** participating States to include an Environmental Accountability Annex while protecting locations, movement, command-and-control, readiness, design and proliferation-sensitive information.

## VII. Implementation and responsibility map

- **Secretary-General / UNODA:** consult on definitions, maintain the public repository, preserve version history and convene interactive review.
- **NPT nuclear-weapon States:** report using the common template and answer technical questions.
- **Non-NPT possessors:** use a parallel voluntary route without creating an NPT obligation.
- **Technical institutions:** contribute only within existing mandates; ordinary IAEA safeguards do not become warhead inspections.
- **Affected communities and independent experts:** provide environmental evidence and assess reported cleanup outcomes, supplementing rather than replacing official data.

The sequence is practical: **common glossary → national report → protected security review → interactive questions → corrected report → environmental outcome update**.

## Japan’s accountability declaration

Japan publicly reports approximately **44.4 tonnes of separated civilian plutonium** as of the end of 2024: **8.6 tonnes held domestically and 35.8 tonnes held abroad**.[11] Japan has published detailed annual data since 1994. All nuclear material in Japan is subject to comprehensive IAEA safeguards and the Additional Protocol; the IAEA reached the broader conclusion for 2024 that all nuclear material in Japan remained in peaceful activities.[11]

This is civilian material, not a warhead stockpile. Japan raises the figure itself because the principle is universal: disclose the category, define it accurately, submit it to the applicable safeguards system and permit informed scrutiny.

## Scope safeguards

This proposal does **not** treat external estimates as official counts, compel operational disclosure, authorize ordinary IAEA inspection of warheads, expose designs or vulnerabilities, impose treaty duties on non-parties, or substitute reporting for actual disarmament.

## Closing

A number without a definition is not transparency. A report without questions is not accountability. An arsenal ledger without its environmental liabilities is not complete. **The world cannot remediate what nuclear possessors refuse to report.**

### Precision declarations

- SIPRI’s 12,187 is an estimate, not an official UN total.
- The US official 3,748 figure is a military-stockpile category dated September 2023; it is not comparable without adjustment to SIPRI’s January 2026 total inventory.
- France, China, Russia, India, Israel and DPRK figures are not stated as official counts where none exists.
- The IAEA is not assigned a universal warhead-inspection mandate.
- Japan’s plutonium declaration concerns separated civilian material, not nuclear weapons.
