**Delegation:** Japan

**Committee:** United Nations General Assembly

**Conference:** CCA MUN ’26

**Agenda:** Assessing the ecological threats of nuclear proliferation on global boundaries

**MOD 1 focus:** Assessing transboundary radiological contamination arising from the legacies of past nuclear testing and production

**Submission mode:** Polished PDF substantive chit

**Version:** 2.0 — expanded detailed edition · 22 August 2026

---

## I. The submission in one paragraph

The debate has named the harm; it has not yet named the evidentiary process required to prove, monitor and remediate it. Japan therefore proposes a **voluntary, consent-based Nuclear Legacy Evidence and Response Mechanism** built on three connected functions — **MONITOR, RESPOND and REMEDIATE** — that convert fragmented national records into a common reporting method, convert contested evidence into a consent-based technical process, and convert expenditure into measurable outcomes. Nothing in the mechanism compels a State, exposes a weapon design, or prejudges legal liability. It is designed precisely so that the States most capable of obstructing progress have no defensible reason to do so.

---

## II. The record already proves that national boundaries are not an adequate safety system

The General Assembly has expressly recognized that the consequences of nuclear-weapons use and testing have transcended national borders, contaminated environments and continued to harm health, food security and development.[1] The history behind that recognition is specific:

- **Marshall Islands.** The United States conducted **67 nuclear tests in the Marshall Islands** between 1946 and 1958.[2]
- **Lucky Dragon No. 5.** On 1 March 1954, the Japanese fishing vessel was operating **well outside the declared United States danger area** when fallout from the Castle Bravo test reached it; all **23 crew members** were exposed.[3] A line drawn around a test area did not contain the radioactive material.
- **Semipalatinsk.** At the former Soviet test site in Kazakhstan, **456 tests** were conducted between 1949 and 1989 — **116 of them above ground** — across a territory of roughly **18,500 square kilometres**.[4] IAEA-linked dose-reconstruction records document fallout plumes extending beyond the test-site boundary.[5]
- **Production legacies.** The United States Government Accountability Office reports that Department of Energy sites remain contaminated from decades of nuclear-weapons production and nuclear-energy research; in May 2025 the Department estimated that remaining Environmental Management cleanup would cost **more than half a trillion dollars**.[6] A retired warhead does not retire its waste, contaminated facilities, soil or groundwater.

Japan does not claim every part of every former site is equally contaminated, or that every test crossed an international border. The evidence instead demonstrates a simpler proposition: **no national boundary functioned as a radiological barrier, so no national archive alone can serve as the final scientific authority.**

### The asymmetric evidentiary burden

Today, the affected State must usually disprove a source State's assessment using evidence the source State controls — records, models, dose calculations and conclusions. That asymmetry is the real loophole in the legacy regime. The Mechanism inverts the burden: **evidence must carry its own label and its own chain of custody before any conclusion is treated as authoritative.**

Every finding produced under the Mechanism is classified into one of three evidence classes:

1. **Measured data** — instrument readings, sampling results, monitoring records;
2. **Modelled estimates** — dose reconstructions, plume calculations, projections, with assumptions disclosed;
3. **Unresolved uncertainty** — data gaps, conflicting records and questions requiring further study.

Labels, not adjectives, decide what the world may rely on.

## III. The institutional gap

Existing institutions are necessary but not interchangeable:

- The **CTBTO** monitoring system detects nuclear explosions; it is not a universal remediation authority.[7]
- **IAEA safeguards** verify peaceful-use commitments; they do not create an automatic right to inspect every former military site.
- **UNSCEAR** evaluates radiation science; it does not administer national cleanup programmes.[8]
- The **IAEA Incident and Emergency Centre and RANET** provide emergency notification and requested assistance; they are not standing legacy-remediation mechanisms.[9]
- The **IAEA ENVIRONET network** shares remediation expertise; it does not compile a universal legacy evidence record.[10]

Meanwhile, testing, dose, plume and production records remain fragmented across national archives, and affected States may lack the laboratories or source data needed to challenge a source State's assessment. The General Assembly can recommend common reporting, request a repository, convene technical cooperation and encourage assistance. It cannot manufacture compulsory access to classified military sites. **Japan's mechanism is designed accordingly — voluntary, layered, and impossible to dismiss as an inspection regime.**

The good news is that the machinery exists to build on: the General Assembly's legacy resolutions (78/240, 79/60, 80/56),[1][11][12] the International Meeting on Victim Assistance and Environmental Remediation to be convened on **1 September 2026** pursuant to resolutions 79/60 and 80/56,[12] the independent **Scientific Panel on the Effects of Nuclear War** established by the General Assembly in December 2023 and appointed in July 2025,[13][14] IAEA safety standards for remediation and emergency preparedness,[15] the Early Notification and Assistance Conventions,[16][17] and the victim-assistance and remediation duties in the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons for its States parties.[18] The Mechanism connects these existing pieces into one evidentiary process — it does not invent a new bureaucracy.

## IV. Japan's three-layer mechanism

### LAYER 1 — MONITOR: Voluntary Nuclear Legacy Environmental Reports

Each participating State would submit a common public annex covering, as applicable, a **twelve-field reporting template**:

1. **Test events** — dates, broad locations, atmospheric or underground classification;
2. **Production facilities** — former and current sites, decommissioning status;
3. **Waste categories and inventory** — types, quantities, management status;
4. **Contaminated media** — known or suspected soil, groundwater, sediments and marine pathways;
5. **Monitoring network and methods** — stations, instruments, sampling protocols;
6. **Dose reconstruction and meteorological records** — declassified data and methods;
7. **Transboundary pathway assessments** — plausible routes across borders, shared waters and food chains;
8. **Remediation status and plans** — completed, ongoing and planned work;
9. **Expenditure and funding sources** — published categories, not commercial secrets;
10. **Timelines and milestones** — published schedules for remaining work;
11. **Measurable completion indicators** — indicators that distinguish activity from outcome;
12. **Uncertainty register** — data gaps, model limitations, version history.

Weapon designs, exact vulnerabilities, operational locations and proliferation-sensitive information remain protected. Measured data, modelled estimates and unresolved uncertainty must be labelled separately. The template would be developed by the Secretary-General in consultation with the IAEA, UNEP, UNSCEAR and the CTBTO Preparatory Commission, acting within their mandates — a non-binding reporting discipline, not a treaty.

### LAYER 2 — RESPOND: Affected-State Technical Review and Notification

**Review.** An affected territorial State could voluntarily request a technical review coordinated through a roster compiled by the Secretary-General, drawing — strictly within existing mandates — on the IAEA, UNSCEAR, CTBTO expertise, regional bodies and qualified national laboratories. The source State would be invited to provide original records and methodology. **No site visit would occur without host-State consent.** Findings would be published with the three-class evidence label.

**Notification.** When newly identified legacy contamination presents a **plausible significant cross-border pathway**, participating States would promptly notify potentially affected neighbours and share sampling methods. Plausibility criteria would include:

- detection of elevated radionuclide concentrations above agreed screening levels;
- model results indicating potential transboundary transport;
- monitored changes at legacy sites; and
- any development a participating State considers potentially significant.

Notification is **before attribution**: it does not predetermine legal liability or political responsibility. The design principle is drawn from the Early Notification Convention — warning must not wait for blame.[16]

### LAYER 3 — REMEDIATE: Voluntary Assistance and Measurable Outcomes

Japan proposes a voluntary technical and financial assistance window with three tracks:

- **Track A — Assessment and monitoring:** laboratories, sampling equipment, training, data interpretation;
- **Track B — Health and victims:** health screening, long-term care, psychosocial support;
- **Track C — Remediation operations:** source control, soil/water/food-chain measures, safe waste management.

**Priority criteria:** affected population size and vulnerability; significance of food and water pathways; limited national capacity; community request; demonstrated need. **Community participation is mandatory in project design and outcome review; donors cannot control scientific conclusions; and no assistance may require an affected State to surrender its legal position.**

**Outcome indicators** — reported publicly and periodically:

- people receiving care and support;
- samples analysed and results published;
- contaminated material secured;
- water and soil trend data;
- access restrictions safely reduced;
- livelihoods restored; and
- completion against published timelines.

### Evidence-to-action control logic

- **Fragmented records** produce incompatible baselines → **MONITOR** creates common categories and labels uncertainty.
- **Contested pathways** delay warnings → **RESPOND** creates consent-based review and notification without predetermining liability.
- **Unmeasured cleanup** turns expenditure into a substitute for outcomes → **REMEDIATE** requires published indicators and community participation.

No layer substitutes for another: evidence without response does not protect neighbours, and remediation without measurement cannot prove success.

## V. Draft-ready committee recommendations

1. **Requests** the Secretary-General, in consultation with relevant international organizations acting within their mandates, to develop a non-binding Nuclear Legacy Environmental Reporting Template and public repository;
2. **Invites** affected States, by consent, to request independent technical review and **encourages** source States to provide declassified test, plume, dose and production records relevant to environmental assessment;
3. **Encourages** participating States to notify potentially affected neighbours promptly where newly identified legacy contamination presents a plausible significant cross-border pathway, without predetermining liability or attribution;
4. **Encourages** voluntary technical and financial assistance through existing mechanisms — including IAEA RANET and ENVIRONET — with affected-community participation, transparent expenditure and periodic outcome reporting;
5. **Recommends** that participating States label environmental findings as measured data, modelled estimates or unresolved uncertainty, with chain-of-custody and confidence information;
6. **Invites** relevant organizations to support regional laboratories, training and joint monitoring exercises for legacy-affected developing States;
7. **Decides** to remain seized of the matter and **requests** the Secretary-General to report on implementation to the General Assembly.

---

## VI. Implementation and responsibility map

- **Secretary-General / UNODA:** consult on the template, maintain the public repository, compile the technical roster, summarize participation without grading political positions.
- **Affected territorial State:** controls consent for site access, requests review, nominates community representatives, approves publication of review findings.
- **Source State:** supplies declassified records, methodology, uncertainty ranges and relevant technical experts.
- **Technical institutions:** contribute only within existing mandates; distinguish measurement, modelling and unresolved uncertainty.
- **Donors and partners:** provide voluntary finance, laboratories, training and remediation expertise without controlling scientific conclusions.

**Sequence of implementation:** common template → voluntary national report → affected-State request → consent-based technical review → notification where pathways are plausible → remediation plan → periodic public outcomes.

---

## VII. Scope safeguards

This proposal does **not** authorize compulsory inspections, determine historical legal liability, expose weapon designs, assume every test crossed an international border, or claim every part of a former test site is equally dangerous. It distinguishes weapons-testing and production legacies from reactor accidents, routine safeguarded civilian operations and current regulated releases. It creates no new organs and no new financial assessments.

---

## VIII. Debate counters

**If a source State says: "Our records are classified."**
> The template asks only for non-sensitive ecological records — dates, plume data, sampling results, monitoring history. Which of those reveals a weapon design? The mechanism was drafted so that classification of operations never justifies classification of consequences.

**If a source State says: "This is a Trojan horse for inspections."**
> The mechanism is consent-based. No site visit occurs without host-State consent. No new inspection authority is created. A review that requires consent is not an inspection regime — it is a reporting discipline.

**If a delegation says: "Voluntary mechanisms change nothing."**
> Every mechanism at the United Nations begins voluntarily. The reporting template, the evidence labels and the outcome indicators create the common language that any future binding instrument will need. Japan asks which delegation prefers no language at all.

**If a delegation says: "The meeting on victim assistance and remediation covers this."**
> The International Meeting of 1 September 2026 is an essential forum for political commitment. It is not a standing evidentiary process for records, notification and measured outcomes. The Mechanism complements the meeting by giving it a method to carry forward.

**If an affected State says: "We lack laboratories and data."**
> That is exactly the asymmetry the Mechanism corrects: Track A assistance, regional laboratories and source-State data release operate precisely where capacity is weakest.

---

## IX. Closing

Radiation must not inherit the secrecy that created it. Japan asks this committee to move from describing inherited contamination to establishing a common method for documenting it, warning affected States and measuring whether remediation actually succeeds. **A line on a map never contained fallout; a method shared by all States can.**

### Precision declarations

- "More than half a trillion dollars" is the DOE estimate reported by GAO for remaining Environmental Management cleanup; it is not attributed exclusively to current warheads.
- "Beyond the test-site boundary" is not presented as proof that every Kazakhstan plume crossed an international border.
- Lucky Dragon is used as evidence that fallout exceeded the declared danger area, not as a claim about every nuclear test.
- The Semipalatinsk site area (roughly 18,500 km²) and test counts (456 total, 116 above ground) follow the IAEA and CTBTO records.
- The International Meeting of 1 September 2026 is referenced as a scheduled process pursuant to resolutions 79/60 and 80/56, not as a completed event.
- The Scientific Panel is referenced as established (December 2023) and appointed (July 2025), with its report anticipated in 2027.
- TPNW Articles 6 and 7 are cited as precedent for States parties, not as universal law.
- GSG-15 is cited as IAEA safety guidance, not as a binding treaty.
- The Early Notification and Assistance Conventions are used as design precedents; the chit does not claim they automatically govern military legacy sites.
- All institutional participation is qualified by consent and existing legal mandates.
