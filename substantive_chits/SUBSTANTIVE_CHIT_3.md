## Exact handwritten submission

National security can justify protecting an operation; it cannot justify hiding every ecological consequence of that operation. Japan therefore proposes a **Narrow Security Exception for Nuclear Environmental Accountability**, expressed through the **SHIELD Standard**.

The legal balance already exists in principle. In its 1996 Nuclear Weapons Advisory Opinion, the International Court of Justice held that environmental obligations were not intended to deprive a State of self-defence, but that environmental considerations must still inform what is necessary and proportionate in pursuit of legitimate military objectives.[1] The General Assembly later took note of the International Law Commission’s principles on protecting the environment before, during and after armed conflict, including prevention, mitigation and remediation.[2]

Japan’s SHIELD Standard asks six questions whenever a State invokes national security to withhold nuclear-environmental information:

1. **Specific security harm:** Has the State identified the category of security harm that disclosure would create, without being required to reveal the secret itself?
2. **Harm beyond borders:** Has it assessed plausible effects on neighbouring States, shared waters, food chains and future generations?
3. **Information minimized, not erased:** Could aggregate, delayed, redacted or protected-channel disclosure address the risk?
4. **Emergency warning preserved:** Are monitoring, regulator communication and rapid notification guaranteed when significant radiological consequences may cross borders?
5. **Lifecycle liabilities reported:** Are waste, contaminated sites, decommissioning and remediation obligations visible even when operational details remain classified?
6. **Decision independently reviewed:** Is the secrecy claim examined by an independent national authority or agreed technical process and reconsidered periodically?

The standard is practical. The IAEA’s Seven Indispensable Pillars and Five Concrete Principles show that armed conflict does not suspend nuclear safety: physical integrity, power, monitoring, staff capacity, logistics and regulator communication remain essential.[3] The Early Notification Convention separately demonstrates that rapid information exchange can protect other States when a covered nuclear accident may cause a significant transboundary release.[4] Japan does not claim that this Convention automatically governs every military activity; it adopts the underlying design principle that warning must not wait for political attribution.

Technical protection is also possible. The UK–Norway Initiative tested managed access and information barriers designed to verify nuclear-warhead dismantlement without revealing national-security or proliferation-sensitive information.[5] Security and accountability are therefore not binary choices.

Japan recommends that this committee:

- **endorses** the SHIELD Standard as a voluntary test for nuclear-security exceptions;
- **encourages** dual environmental assessments: a protected technical assessment and a public aggregate summary;
- **calls for** independent review, regional warning channels and periodic reporting across the nuclear-policy lifecycle—before deployment decisions, during operation and after retirement or decommissioning.

**The governing rule is narrow and defensible: classify the operation, not the consequence; protect the secret, not the pollution.**

## Supporting architecture

### The three-stage nuclear-policy lifecycle

#### BEFORE — Anticipate

- Conduct a security-screened environmental risk assessment.
- Identify plausible transboundary pathways and emergency contacts.
- Separate public aggregate findings from a protected technical annex.
- Establish financial and institutional responsibility for later decommissioning and remediation.

#### DURING — Monitor and warn

- Maintain independent regulatory communication and environmental monitoring.
- Preserve facility staffing, power, cooling, logistics and emergency preparedness.
- Notify potentially affected States when a significant cross-border radiological consequence is plausible.
- Use protected technical channels before withholding information entirely.

#### AFTER — Account and remediate

- Report aggregate waste, contaminated sites, decommissioning progress and environmental liabilities.
- Review whether continued secrecy remains necessary.
- Include affected communities in remediation indicators and outcome review.
- Preserve records needed for long-term health and environmental assessment.

### Draft-ready recommendations

1. **Recommends** the voluntary SHIELD Standard—specificity, transboundary-harm assessment, least-restrictive disclosure, preserved warning, lifecycle reporting and independent review—for evaluating national-security exceptions in nuclear environmental policy;
2. **Encourages** States to prepare a protected technical environmental assessment and a public aggregate summary for security-sensitive nuclear policies and facilities;
3. **Calls upon** States to maintain independent regulation, regional warning channels, record preservation and periodic environmental reporting throughout deployment, operation, retirement, decommissioning and remediation.

### Scope safeguards

The proposal does not compel classified disclosure, weaken self-defence rights, predetermine the legality of nuclear deterrence, create new inspection powers, disclose weapon design or location, or claim that civilian safety conventions automatically apply to every military activity. It creates a voluntary proportionality and accountability discipline for deciding what may remain secret and what ecological information must still be protected, reviewed or shared.

### Precision declarations

- The ICJ did not hold that environmental law abolishes self-defence; the submission accurately states the Court’s balance.
- General Assembly resolution 77/104 took note of the ILC draft principles; it did not convert every principle into a universally binding treaty rule.
- The IAEA pillars and principles are used as nuclear-safety policy evidence, not as a universal enforcement mandate.
- The Early Notification Convention is used as a design precedent; the submission expressly rejects automatic extension to every military activity.
- The UK–Norway Initiative concerned research and exercises, not a currently binding universal inspection regime.
