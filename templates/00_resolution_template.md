# RESOLUTION TEMPLATE — MUN COMMAND CENTER

Formatting law (varies slightly by conference — check the RoP; these are the common rules):
- One long sentence: preambulatory clauses end with **commas**; operative clauses end with **semicolons**; the final operative clause ends with a **period**.
- Preambulatory phrases are typically **underlined or italicized**; operative verbs are typically **underlined or bolded** — follow the conference style.
- Operative clauses are **numbered** (1., 2., 3.); sub-clauses are lettered (a), b), c)); sub-sub clauses numbered (i), ii), iii)).
- Single-spaced, blank line between clauses; indent operative clauses and sub-clauses.
- Acronyms written out in full on first use.

---

```text
COMMITTEE: [Full Official Committee Name]
TOPIC: [Exact agenda wording as given by the conference]
SPONSORS: [Delegations that will defend the draft]
SIGNATORIES: [Delegations willing to vote yes]

The [Committee/Organ],

[Preambulatory clause 1: justify action — reference the problem, its scale, and why the committee must act],
[Preambulatory clause 2: cite prior UN action — correct document symbols, e.g. recalling General Assembly resolution A/RES/70/1],
[Preambulatory clause 3: cite relevant treaty/international law if any],
[Preambulatory clause 4: acknowledge work of states, agencies, or regional organizations],
[Preambulatory clause 5: define terms or set the tone],

1. [Operative clause: first verb + action; one idea per clause — who, what, under what authority, with what resources, by when];
2. [Operative clause];
   a) [sub-clause detail];
   b) [sub-clause detail];
3. [Operative clause establishing a mechanism — name the actor, mandate, and reporting line];
4. [Operative clause on funding — donor base, fund manager, criteria];
5. [Operative clause on monitoring/review — indicators, report to whom, review date];
6. [Operative clause on safeguards — sovereignty, consent, data protection, capacity differentiation];
7. [Final operative clause: decide the follow-up — e.g., "Decides to remain seized of the matter."]
```

## Operative Clause Test (before you finalize each clause)

1. Who acts? 2. What do they do? 3. Under what authority? 4. With what resources? 5. By when? 6. How is progress reviewed? 7. What safeguards protect sovereignty/consent/rights?

If a clause cannot answer all seven, it is a statement, not a mechanism — rewrite it.

## Sponsor and Signatory Math

- **Sponsors** defend the draft; they are counted in thresholds at many conferences — recruit a comfortable majority of active sponsors before introduction.
- **Signatories** only pledge to vote yes — collect them broadly; a long signatory list reads as consensus.
- Never list a delegation as sponsor or signatory without their explicit consent.

## Clause Architecture (order of strength — ladder your verbs)

Weak → strong: Encourages → Recommends → Calls upon → Requests → Urges → Strongly urges → Demands (SC only, and only when enforceable).

Use the strongest verb you can defend, the weakest you need. In a GA resolution, "demands" without enforcement capacity is a rhetorical trap — opponents will quote it back at you.

## Poison-Pill Self-Check

- Does any clause create an obligation your own country could never accept if roles were reversed?
- Is every term ("voluntary", "binding", "monitoring", "assistance") defined or bounded so a hostile amendment cannot reinterpret it?
- Which clause is the most attackable — and is the defense line prepared?

## Amendment-Friendly Design

- Keep each operative clause to one idea (splitting a multi-idea clause invites division of the question).
- Put your red lines in clauses with clear mechanisms (harder to delete) rather than in aspirational language.
- Pre-write softer versions of your own clauses for negotiation — offering a pre-drafted compromise beats improvising one.
